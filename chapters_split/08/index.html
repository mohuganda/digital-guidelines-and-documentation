    Chapter 8: Endocrine and Metabolic Diseases¶

    8.1 Endocrine and Metabolic Conditions¶

    8.1.1 Addison’s Disease¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E27.1–E27.4

    Addison’s disease is a condition where the adrenal gland produces insufficient glucocorticoid hormones. This is also called adrenal insufficiency.

    Causes

    • Abrupt cessation of steroid treatment after long-term use, which is the most common cause
    • Autoimmune destruction of the adrenal gland
    • Tuberculosis of the adrenal glands
    • HIV/AIDS
    • Surgical removal of the adrenal glands
    • Cancer affecting the adrenal glands
    • Bleeding into the adrenal glands
    • Necrosis of the adrenal glands

    Clinical features

    Acute adrenal insufficiency may present with:

    • Weakness and easy fatigability
    • Shock
    • Very low blood pressure
    • Hypoglycaemic attacks
    • Mental changes, such as irritability and restlessness, progressing to coma
    • Fever
    • Hyponatraemia, or low sodium
    • Hyperkalaemia, or high potassium
    • Acidosis

    Chronic adrenal insufficiency may present with the above features plus:

    • Weight loss
    • Hair loss
    • Darkening of the skin and mouth
    • Menstrual disturbance
    • Infertility

    Symptoms are worse during stress, such as infections.

    Differential diagnosis

    • HIV/AIDS
    • Tuberculosis
    • Cancer
    • Depression
    • Diabetes mellitus
    • Hypothyroidism

    Investigations

    • Drug history
    • Refer to a higher level for hormone tests if there is no clear history of abrupt withdrawal of steroid treatment

    Management

    Clinical situation Treatment LOC
    Acute crisis Give hydrocortisone 100 mg IV every 6 hours until stable, then switch to oral treatment. H
    Acute crisis in children aged 0 to 3 years Give hydrocortisone 25 mg. H
    Acute crisis in children aged 3 to 12 years Give hydrocortisone 50 mg. H
    Acute crisis Give IV fluids and dextrose to maintain normal volume and blood sugar. H
    Acute crisis Treat complications and concomitant illnesses, such as infections. H
    Chronic case Give hydrocortisone tablets 10 mg. H
    Abrupt steroid cessation Restart prednisolone treatment, then slowly decrease the dose by 2.5 to 5 mg per week. H
    Replacement treatment Give prednisolone 5 to 7.5 mg/day. H
    Replacement treatment in children Give prednisolone 1 to 5 mg/day. H
    Stress situations, such as surgery, disease, or labour Use doses as in acute regimens. H

    Prevention

    • Avoid self-medication with steroids, such as prednisolone and dexamethasone.
    • Decrease steroids gradually if used for treatment durations longer than 2 weeks.

    8.1.2 Cushing’s Syndrome¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E24

    Cushing’s syndrome is a constellation of signs and symptoms caused by chronic glucocorticoid, or steroid, excess. It may result from excessive secretion, but is more commonly caused by chronic glucocorticoid therapy.

    Causes

    • Iatrogenic causes, especially steroid treatment
    • Cushing’s disease
    • Adrenal adenoma
    • Adrenal carcinoma

    Clinical features

    • Central or truncal obesity
    • Moon face
    • Buffalo hump
    • Thinning of the skin
    • Striae
    • Poor wound healing
    • Muscle weakness and atrophy
    • Hirsutism and acne in females
    • Hypertension
    • Hyperglycaemia

    Differential diagnosis

    • Ordinary obesity
    • Alcoholism, including alcohol-induced pseudo-Cushing’s syndrome

    Investigations

    • Drug history
    • Refer to a higher level for hormonal tests, such as dexamethasone suppression test, if there is no history of steroid overuse

    Management

    Clinical situation Treatment LOC
    Iatrogenic Cushing’s syndrome Slowly decrease the steroid dose by 2.5 to 5 mg every 1 to 2 weeks. H
    Non-iatrogenic Cushing’s syndrome Refer to a higher level of care. RR
    Iatrogenic Cushing’s syndrome with complications Refer to a higher level of care. RR

    8.1.3 Diabetes Mellitus¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E08–E13

    Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disease resulting from insulin insufficiency or ineffectiveness. This may be due to decreased insulin secretion, peripheral resistance to the action of insulin, or a combination of both.

    Types of diabetes mellitus

    • Type 1 diabetes: decreased insulin production due to autoimmune destruction of the pancreas. It usually starts at a young age.
    • Type 2 diabetes: insulin resistance, usually combined with insufficient insulin production as the disease progresses. It usually starts in adulthood.
    • Gestational diabetes: any degree of glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy.
    • Secondary diabetes: diabetes due to other identifiable causes, such as Cushing’s syndrome or chronic pancreatitis.

    Causes and risk factors

    Type 1 diabetes risk factors include:

    • Genetic factors
    • Environmental factors, such as some viral infections

    Type 2 diabetes risk factors include:

    Non-modifiable risk factors

    • Age above 40 years
    • Family history in first-degree relatives
    • History of gestational diabetes mellitus
    • Delivery of a big baby weighing more than 4 kg

    Modifiable risk factors

    • Unhealthy diet
    • Physical inactivity
    • Tobacco use
    • Harmful use of alcohol
    • Hypertension
    • Stress
    • Obesity
    • High cholesterol levels
    • Impaired glucose tolerance

    Clinical features

    Classical symptoms include:

    • Polyuria, meaning frequent urination or waking at night to urinate
    • Polydipsia, meaning frequent thirst or drinking a lot of water
    • Polyphagia, meaning increased appetite, feeling hungry all the time, or frequent eating
    • Polyneuropathy, including burning pains, pins and needles, or numbness

    Other symptoms include:

    • Weight loss despite high appetite
    • Frequent skin infections, such as boils
    • Itchy genitalia, such as candidiasis
    • Slow-healing wounds
    • Fatigue or feeling tired all the time
    • Children not wanting to play
    • Bed-wetting in children
    • Poor vision
    • Presentation with complications

    Type 2 diabetes often presents with minor non-specific symptoms. It may be diagnosed through screening or when the patient presents with complications.

    Complications

    Acute complications include:

    • Coma due to diabetic ketoacidosis
    • Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemia
    • Hypoglycaemia

    See section 8.1.4 and section 1.1.6.

    Chronic complications include:

    • Microvascular complications affecting small blood vessels, including:
      • Retinopathy
      • Nephropathy
      • Neuropathy
    • Macrovascular complications affecting large blood vessels, including:
      • Stroke
      • Ischaemic heart disease
      • Peripheral artery disease
      • Heart attack
    • Kidney failure
    • Blindness
    • Impotence
    • Peripheral neuropathy
    • Diabetic foot, which may lead to amputation

    Differential diagnosis

    • Diabetes insipidus
    • HIV/AIDS
    • Tuberculosis

    Investigations

    • Blood glucose:
      • Fasting blood glucose
      • Random blood glucose
      • Two-hour blood glucose after 75 g glucose
    • Urine testing for glucose
    • Urine testing for ketones, especially in type 1 diabetes
    • HbA1c, or glycated haemoglobin A1c
    • Renal function tests
    • Lipid profile
    • ECG
    • Urine protein or microalbuminuria

    Diagnostic criteria

    Diabetes mellitus may be diagnosed using any of the following:

    • Fasting blood sugar above 7.0 mmol/L, or 126 mg/dL
    • Two-hour blood sugar after 75 g glucose above 11.1 mmol/L, or 200 mg/dL
    • HbA1c above 6.5%
    • In a patient with classical symptoms of hyperglycaemia, random blood sugar above 11.1 mmol/L, or 200 mg/dL

    Caution

    In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycaemia, meaning very high blood sugar levels, criteria 1 to 3 should be confirmed by repeated testing.

    One single slightly elevated blood sugar in the absence of symptoms is not diagnostic for diabetes.

    General management

    The goals of treatment are:

    • Treatment of hyperglycaemia
    • Treatment of associated risk factors
    • Prevention and treatment of acute and chronic complications
    Management area Treatment LOC
    Lifestyle modification Advise a diabetic diet. See section 19.1.3. HC2
    Lifestyle modification Advise weight loss if overweight. HC2
    Lifestyle modification Encourage regular physical exercise. HC2
    Lifestyle modification Advise moderate alcohol intake or no alcohol intake. HC2
    Lifestyle modification Support smoking cessation. HC2
    Risk factor management Assess for other risk factors, such as hypertension, obesity, and smoking, and manage accordingly. HC2
    Hypertension Target blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg. First-line medicines are ACE inhibitors because of renal protection, such as enalapril. See section 4.1.6. HC4
    Dyslipidaemia Consider statin treatment, such as atorvastatin 20 to 40 mg once daily or simvastatin 20 to 40 mg once daily in the evening. H
    Dyslipidaemia Statin treatment is especially indicated if ischaemic heart disease or cerebrovascular disease is already present, or if the patient is above 40 years. H

    Caution

    Do not use beta blockers, such as atenolol, in diabetes.

    Management of complications

    Complication area Treatment LOC
    Screening and assessment Assess for complications, including renal disease, eye problems, diabetic foot, peripheral neuropathy, heart problems, and stroke. Refer or treat accordingly. HC3
    Ischaemic heart disease or stroke Give aspirin 75 to 100 mg daily. HC3
    Peripheral neuropathy Give amitriptyline 10 to 25 mg at night, up to a maximum of 100 mg in divided doses. H
    Ischaemic heart disease or stroke Give atorvastatin 20 to 40 mg once daily. H

    Treatment targets

    • Fasting blood sugar below 7 mmol/L
    • Postprandial blood sugar below 10 mmol/L
    • HbA1c below 7%
    • HbA1c below 7.5% in elderly patients

    Elderly people are at higher risk of hypoglycaemia. Monitor carefully and do not aim for very strict blood sugar control.

    Management of type 1 diabetes

    Treatment LOC
    Give insulin subcutaneously at 0.6 to 1.5 IU/kg/day. HC4
    Children below 5 years: start with insulin 0.5 IU/kg/day and refer to a paediatrician. HC4

    Common insulin preparations

    Type of insulin Usual protocol Onset Peak Duration
    Short-acting regular soluble insulin, such as Actrapid Three times daily, 30 minutes before meals 30 minutes 2 to 5 hours 5 to 8 hours
    Rapid-acting insulin analogue, such as insulin aspart Three times daily, 10 to 15 minutes before meals 10 to 20 minutes 45 minutes 3 to 5 hours
    Intermediate-acting insulin, NPH, such as Insulatard Once or twice daily, evening with or without morning dose 1 to 3 hours 6 to 12 hours 16 to 24 hours
    Biphasic insulin, mixture of regular and NPH, such as Mixtard 30/70 Once or twice daily 30 minutes 2 to 12 hours 16 to 24 hours

    Preferably, a combination of intermediate-acting and short-acting insulin should be used.

    Examples of regimens include:

    • Pre-meal short-acting insulin, such as Actrapid, or rapid-acting insulin analogue, such as insulin aspart, plus evening intermediate-acting insulin, such as Insulatard, or long-acting insulin analogue, such as glargine.
    • The evening dose should be 40 to 50% of the daily dose. This is basal-bolus therapy.

    Alternatively:

    • Give twice-daily premixed insulin, such as Mixtard.
    • Usually give two-thirds of the total dose in the morning and one-third in the evening, 30 minutes before meals.
    • For biphasic insulin aspart, give two-thirds of the total dose in the morning and one-third in the evening, 10 to 15 minutes before meals.

    Note

    Patients on insulin should measure their blood glucose at least twice daily, before breakfast and before dinner, and insulin doses should be adjusted accordingly.

    8.1.4 Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) and Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS)¶

    ICD-10 CODES: E10.1 / E11.0

    Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state are acute metabolic complications of diabetes mellitus.

    Diabetic ketoacidosis is characterised by:

    • Ketosis
    • Acidosis
    • Hyperglycaemia

    It is more common in type 1 diabetes.

    Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state is characterised by:

    • Hyperglycaemia
    • Severe dehydration
    • Hypovolaemia
    • No ketosis
    • No acidosis

    It is more common in type 2 diabetes.

    Causes and precipitating factors

    • Newly diagnosed diabetes
    • Poor control of diabetes mellitus
    • Treatment interruptions
    • Infections
    • Trauma

    Clinical features

    Diabetic ketoacidosis may present with:

    • Acute onset, usually within 24 hours or less
    • Excessive thirst
    • Excessive fluid intake
    • Frequent urination
    • Weight loss
    • Tiredness
    • Abdominal pain
    • Vomiting
    • Altered consciousness
    • Coma
    • Deep acidotic breathing
    • Sweet acetone smell on the breath due to ketosis
    • Cardiovascular collapse with hypotension

    Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state may present with:

    • Slower onset
    • More severe dehydration and fluid deficit
    • No ketosis or acidosis
    • No or few ketones in urine

    Differential diagnosis

    • Other causes of ketoacidosis or hyperglycaemia
    • Other causes of acute abdominal pain
    • Other causes of coma

    Investigations

    • Blood sugar
    • Urine analysis for ketones
    • Full blood count
    • Renal function tests
    • Electrolytes, especially sodium and potassium

    Management

    Management area Treatment LOC
    General measures Monitor blood pressure, urine output, and blood sugar hourly. HC4
    General measures Insert urinary catheter if unconscious. HC4
    General measures Treat infections if present because they may be a precipitating factor. HC4
    Prevention of thromboembolism Give enoxaparin 4,000 IU SC until the patient is able to move. H
    Fluid replacement Give normal saline, NaCl 0.9%. HC4
    Fluid replacement Give 15 to 20 mL/kg in the first hour, usually 500 to 1,000 mL. HC4
    Fluid replacement in children Give 10 to 20 mL/kg. HC4
    Ongoing fluid replacement Continue with 5 to 15 mL/kg/hour according to vital signs, urine output, and clinical condition. HC4
    Dextrose switch If blood sugar is below 14 mmol/L, switch to dextrose 5% if ketones are still present and/or the clinical condition is not yet normal, such as when the patient is unable to eat. HC4
    Insulin Give soluble insulin 4 to 6 IU IM every hour until the condition stabilises. HC4
    Insulin in children above 5 years Give soluble insulin 0.1 IU/kg/hour. HC4
    Insulin in children below 5 years Give soluble insulin 0.05 IU/kg/hour. HC4
    Insulin continuation Continue insulin until ketosis resolves and the patient is able to eat. HC4
    Transition to SC insulin Once the clinical condition normalises, including normal blood pressure, consciousness, urine output, and ability to eat, start the SC insulin regimen. See section 8.1.3. Start SC insulin 1 to 2 hours before stopping IM insulin. HC4

    Potassium replacement

    If potassium level is not available:

    Situation Treatment LOC
    Potassium level not available Add potassium chloride 1 ampoule to every 1 litre of infusion as soon as the patient has started passing urine. HC4

    If potassium levels are available:

    Potassium level Treatment LOC
    K⁺ below 3.5 mmol/L Add 40 mmol potassium chloride, equivalent to 2 ampoules, per 1 litre of fluid. HC4
    K⁺ 3.5 to 5.5 mmol/L Add 20 mmol potassium chloride, equivalent to 1 ampoule, per 1 litre of infusion. HC4
    K⁺ above 5.5 mmol/L Do not add potassium. HC4

    Prevention

    • Early detection of diabetes
    • Good control of diabetes
    • Prompt treatment of infections
    • General patient education

    Hypoglycaemia

    For hypoglycaemia in patients on antidiabetic medicines, manage as described in section 1.1.6.

    For severe hypoglycaemia in diabetes patients treated with insulin who are unconscious or cannot take sugar by mouth:

    Treatment LOC
    Give glucagon 1 mg, equivalent to 1 unit, IM or SC. HC4
    Repeat every 15 minutes once or twice according to response. HC4

    8.1.5 Goitre¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E04

    Goitre is visible enlargement of the thyroid gland. It may or may not be associated with abnormal thyroid function, such as hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism.

    Causes

    • Iodine deficiency
    • Graves’ disease
    • Thyroiditis
    • Multinodular goitre
    • Physiological causes, such as pregnancy or puberty

    Clinical features

    • Visible neck swelling
    • Rarely, difficulty in swallowing

    Investigations

    • Thyroid hormone tests
    • Neck ultrasound

    Management

    Clinical situation Treatment LOC
    Goitre Refer for thyroid hormone testing and specialist management. RR
    Goitre associated with hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism Manage according to the relevant thyroid disorder section. RR
    Goitre causing obstruction Surgery is indicated. RR

    8.1.6 Hyperthyroidism¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E05

    Hyperthyroidism is a condition resulting from excess thyroid hormones, usually due to excessive production.

    Causes

    • Graves’ disease, which is autoimmune and common in females
    • Neonatal thyrotoxicosis
    • Thyroid gland tumours, such as adenomas
    • Multinodular toxic goitre
    • Thyroiditis
    • Iatrogenic causes, such as side effects of some medicines

    Clinical features

    • Weight loss with increased appetite
    • Swelling in the neck, or goitre
    • Palpitations
    • Tachycardia
    • Irritability
    • Nervousness
    • Inability to rest or sleep
    • Irregular or scanty menstrual periods
    • Profuse sweating
    • Extreme discomfort in hot weather
    • High blood pressure
    • Protruding eyes, or exophthalmos, in some forms
    • Frequent defecation

    Differential diagnosis

    • Anxiety states
    • Tumours of the adrenal gland, such as pheochromocytoma
    • Other causes of weight loss
    • Other causes of protruding eyes

    Investigations

    • Blood levels of thyroid hormones, showing high T3 and T4 with low TSH
    • Thyroid ultrasound scan
    • Thyroid gland biopsy for cytology or histology

    Management

    The aim of treatment is to restore the euthyroid state.

    Use pulse rate and thyroid hormone levels to monitor progress.

    Treatment LOC
    Give carbimazole 15 to 40 mg daily, maximum 60 mg, in 2 to 3 divided doses for 1 to 2 months. H
    Children: give carbimazole 750 micrograms/kg/day in divided doses, maximum 30 mg/day. H
    Adjust the dose according to thyroid hormone levels, under specialist management only. H
    To control excessive sympathetic symptoms, such as palpitations, add propranolol 40 to 80 mg every 12 hours for at least 1 month. H
    Children: give propranolol 250 to 500 micrograms/kg 3 to 4 times daily. H
    Once the patient is euthyroid, stop propranolol and progressively reduce carbimazole to a daily maintenance dose of 5 to 15 mg. H
    Continue carbimazole for at least 18 months. H
    Surgery may be required in selected cases, such as obstruction, intolerance, or lack of response to medicine treatment. RR
    Radioactive iodine may be used, especially in toxic multinodular goitre. RR

    Caution

    Patients treated with carbimazole should be advised to report any sore throat immediately because of the rare complication of agranulocytosis, which is a low white blood cell count.

    8.1.7 Hypothyroidism¶

    ICD-10 CODE: E03

    Hypothyroidism is a condition resulting from thyroid hormone deficiency. It is more common in females than in males.

    Causes

    • Autoimmune disease
    • Post-therapeutic causes, especially after radiotherapy or surgical treatment for hyperthyroidism
    • Secondary causes due to congenital enzyme defects
    • Iodine deficiency
    • Iatrogenic causes, such as side effects of some medicines

    Clinical features

    • Dull facial expression
    • Puffiness
    • Periorbital swelling
    • Hoarse voice
    • Slow speech
    • Weight gain
    • Drooping eyelids
    • Sparse, coarse, and dry hair
    • Dry, scaly, and thick skin
    • Forgetfulness
    • Other signs of mental impairment
    • Gradual personality change
    • Bradycardia
    • Constipation, often
    • Anaemia, often
    • Paraesthesia, or numbness, of hands and feet

    Differential diagnosis

    • Myasthenia gravis
    • Depression

    Investigations

    • Blood levels of thyroid hormones, showing low T3 and T4 with high TSH

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Give levothyroxine. H
    Initial dose: levothyroxine 50 to 100 micrograms once daily before breakfast. H
    Elderly patients: start with levothyroxine 50 micrograms once daily. H
    Gradually increase by 25 to 50 micrograms every 4 weeks to a maintenance dose of 100 to 200 micrograms daily, according to hormonal levels. H
    Once stable, check hormone levels every 6 to 12 months. H
    Children: refer for specialist management. H

    Note

    In most cases, treatment is for life.

    Prevention

    • Educate patients on the use of iodised salt.

    8.1.8 Central Precocious Puberty¶

    Central precocious puberty is also referred to as gonadotropin-dependent precocious puberty. It is an endocrine-related developmental disease characterised by the onset of pubertal changes, with development of secondary sexual characteristics and accelerated growth and bone maturation, before the normal age of puberty.

    The normal age of puberty is 8 years in girls and 9 years in boys.

    Causes

    • Premature activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis
    • Idiopathic causes
    • Secondary causes, including:
      • Brain tumours such as glioma and astrocytoma
      • CNS infections such as meningitis and encephalitis
      • Brain malformations such as hydrocephalus and arachnoid cysts
      • Trauma
      • Injuries

    Clinical features

    • Accelerated growth and bone maturation
    • Premature breast development
    • Early menarche in girls
    • Testicular and penile enlargement in boys
    • Development of facial and sexual hair in boys

    Genetic counselling

    Most cases are sporadic. Familial cases show autosomal dominant mode of transmission with incomplete, gender-dependent penetrance.

    Differential diagnosis

    • Gonadotropin-independent precocious puberty
    • McCune-Albright syndrome
    • Gonadal tumours
    • Benign premature thelarche

    Investigations

    • Pelvic ultrasound
    • Screening of basal luteinizing hormone (LH) levels
    • Measurement of gonadotropin levels after stimulation tests using gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Treat progressive central precocious puberty using GnRH agonists, such as leuprolide acetate for depot suspension. NH
    Give leuprolide acetate for depot suspension 7.5 mg injection monthly. NH

    Prognosis

    The disease has minimal consequences during adulthood, although the association of variation of pubertal timing with adult disease or behaviour may be questioned.
