    6.1 Gastrointestinal Emergencies¶

    6.1.1 Appendicitis (Acute)¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K35–K37

    Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix.

    Causes

    • Blockage of the appendix duct with stool or particles, followed by infection by intestinal bacteria

    Clinical features

    • Constipation, commonly
    • Pain situated around the umbilicus
    • Crampy pain that keeps increasing in severity
    • After some hours, the pain localises in the right iliac fossa and becomes continuous
    • Nausea and vomiting may occur
    • Fever, usually low-grade in the initial stages
    • Tenderness and rigidity, also called guarding, in the right iliac fossa
    • Generalised abdominal pain and signs of peritonitis may follow rupture when the contents are poured into the abdominal cavity

    Differential diagnosis

    • Salpingitis in females
    • Ovarian cyst
    • Ectopic pregnancy
    • Pyelonephritis
    • Ureteritis
    • Intestinal obstruction
    • Peptic or duodenal ulcers

    Investigations

    • No special investigations are required if there is a good history and physical examination.
    • Complete blood count to look for leucocytosis
    • Transabdominal ultrasound
    • Abdominal X-ray to assess for perforation and intestinal occlusion

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Emergency surgery. H
    If surgery is delayed, start antibiotic treatment while referring. H
    Give ceftriaxone 2 g IV once daily. H
    Children: ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg IV once daily. H
    Plus metronidazole 500 mg IV every 8 hours. H
    Children: metronidazole 10 mg/kg IV every 8 hours. H
    Start antibiotic prophylaxis before surgery and continue for a duration depending on the findings. Use less than 24 hours for an unperforated appendix and at least 5 days for a perforated appendix. H

    6.1.2 Acute Pancreatitis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K85

    Acute pancreatitis is acute inflammation of the pancreas.

    Causes

    • Excessive alcohol intake
    • Gallstones or biliary tract disease, including obstructive cancer or anatomical abnormalities
    • Infections, such as mumps, HIV, hepatitis A, and ascariasis
    • Medicines or toxic substances, such as sulphonamides, furosemide, lamivudine, analgesics, and organophosphate poisoning
    • Peptic or duodenal ulcers

    Clinical features

    • Acute abdominal pain, usually in the epigastrium, radiating to the back
    • Pain worsened by eating or lying down and relieved by sitting up or leaning forward
    • Nausea
    • Vomiting
    • Abdominal distension
    • Fever
    • Tachycardia
    • Dehydration
    • Severe illness with shock may occur
    • Very tender abdomen, but in the absence of peritonitis there is no rigidity or rebound tenderness

    Complications

    • Pseudocysts
    • Necrotising pancreatitis with infection
    • Peritonitis

    Differential diagnosis

    • Perforated peptic ulcer
    • Peritonitis
    • Acute cholecystitis or inflammation of the biliary tract
    • Sickle-cell anaemia crisis

    Investigations

    • Serum amylase
    • Complete blood count
    • Random blood sugar
    • Pancreatic amylase and lipase raised more than 3 times normal
    • Ultrasound to assess for gallstones, pancreatic oedema, and abdominal fluid
    • Liver function tests, which may show raised liver enzymes

    Management of mild acute pancreatitis

    Mild acute pancreatitis is pancreatitis with no organ failure, no local or systemic complications, no signs of peritonitis, normal serum creatinine, and normal haematocrit.

    Treatment LOC
    Start early aggressive fluid resuscitation and correct acid-base balance. HC4
    Prevent volume depletion using adequate fluids with Ringer’s lactate. Give 5 to 10 mL/kg/hour, or 250 to 500 mL of isotonic crystalloids in the first 12 to 24 hours, aiming for urine output of at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour. HC4
    Give IV fluids to correct metabolic and electrolyte disturbances and prevent hypovolaemia and hypotension. HC4
    Monitor electrolytes. HC4
    Aim to decrease haematocrit and blood urea nitrogen within 48 hours. Evaluate every 4 to 6 hours. HC4

    Pain control

    Treatment LOC
    Give analgesia using opioids, paracetamol, or epidural anaesthesia. Avoid NSAIDs. HC4
    Give rectal or IV paracetamol 500 mg every 6 to 8 hours. HC4
    Alternatively, give pethidine 25 to 100 mg SC or IM, or 25 to 50 mg slow IV. Repeat as needed every 4 to 6 hours. HC4
    Alternatively, give IV morphine 1 to 3 mg every 4 hours. HC4
    Monitor for complications of opioids, including constipation, dysphagia, respiratory depression, and confusion. HC4

    Management of vomiting and ileus

    Treatment LOC
    Give anti-emetics as appropriate. HC4
    Give metoclopramide 10 mg IV or IM every 8 hours. HC4
    Pass a nasogastric tube for suction when persistent vomiting or ileus occurs. HC4

    Feeding and nutrition

    Treatment LOC
    Do not give feeds by mouth until signs and symptoms of acute inflammation subside, including cessation of abdominal tenderness and pain, return of hunger, and general well-being. HC4
    Provide energy with dextrose 50%, 300 to 500 mL per day. Add 50 mL to 500 mL normal saline to prevent muscle wasting. HC4
    Start early oral re-feeding on demand within 48 to 72 hours, as soon as the patient is able and can tolerate feeds. HC4
    Start with clear liquids, then low-fat semi-solid feeds, then a normal diet according to tolerance. HC4
    Monitor vital signs, fluid intake, urinary output, and gastrointestinal symptoms daily. HC4
    If oral feeding is not possible, consider peripheral or central parenteral nutrition. HC4

    Glycaemic control

    Treatment LOC
    Hyperglycaemia is common. Keep serum blood sugar between 6 and 9 mmol/L. HC4
    Avoid hypoglycaemia. HC4

    Antibiotics

    Treatment LOC
    Avoid inappropriate antibiotic use and other unnecessary medicines, including prophylactic antibiotics. HC4
    If there is a specific infection, such as biliary sepsis, pulmonary infection, or UTI, treat vigorously with appropriate antibiotic therapy. HC4

    Other measures

    Treatment LOC
    Address the underlying cause as appropriate. HC4
    Stop alcohol or causative medicines. HC4
    Mobilise the patient when clinically appropriate. HC4
    Evaluate for gallstones using ultrasound scan. HC4
    Manage complications, such as acute peri-pancreatic fluid collections, acute necrosis, and pseudocyst. HC4

    Moderately acute pancreatitis

    Moderately acute pancreatitis includes:

    • Transient organ failure lasting less than 48 hours
    • Local or systemic complications without persistent organ failure
    Treatment LOC
    Treat as above and refer or consult a specialist at a higher level of care. RR

    Severe acute pancreatitis

    Severe acute pancreatitis includes:

    • Persistent organ failure lasting more than 48 hours
    • Single or multiple organ failure
    Treatment LOC
    Treat as above and refer or consult a specialist at a higher level of care. RR
    Admit to HDU or ICU for monitoring and nursing care where available. RR
    Continue volume resuscitation. RR
    Provide pain management. RR
    Provide nutrition and re-feeding support. RR
    Maintain glycaemic control. RR
    Insert a nasogastric tube where indicated. RR
    Give oxygen or mechanical ventilation where indicated. RR
    Provide renal replacement therapy where indicated. RR
    Address the cause where possible. RR
    Manage complications as appropriate, including acute peri-pancreatic fluid collection, acute necrosis, and pseudocyst. RR

    Note

    Look out for diabetes mellitus as a consequence of damage to the pancreas.

    Prevention

    • Reduce alcohol intake to moderate consumption
    • Limit use of toxic medicines or substances

    6.1.3 Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding¶

    Upper gastrointestinal bleeding is bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract, including the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum. It can be a medical emergency.

    Causes

    • Gastro-oesophageal varices
    • Peptic ulcer disease
    • Severe gastritis
    • Cancer
    • Mallory-Weiss tear, which is a tear in the oesophageal mucosa caused by forceful retching

    Clinical features

    • Vomiting of fresh blood, also called haematemesis
    • Coffee-brown vomitus, caused by degraded blood mixed with stomach contents
    • Melaena, which is passage of soft, dark red, smelly stool
    • Black stool in minor bleeding

    Complications

    • Acute hypovolaemia, especially if bleeding is acute and abundant
    • Syncope
    • Hypotension
    • Tachycardia
    • Sweating
    • Chronic anaemia if blood loss is subacute or chronic

    Diagnosis

    • Endoscopy

    6.1.4 Peritonitis¶

    Peritonitis is inflammation of the peritoneum. It may result from bacterial contamination, perforation of abdominal organs, chemical irritation, or spread of infection within the abdominal cavity.

    Causes

    Bacterial causes include:

    • Ruptured appendix
    • Perforated peptic ulcer
    • Perforated bowel due to obstruction or injury
    • Perforation of the gall bladder containing infected bile
    • Perforation of the uterus
    • Tuberculosis
    • Abscess
    • Typhoid ulcers
    • Malignancy
    • Post-operative peritonitis

    Chemical causes include leakage of the following into the peritoneal cavity:

    • Urine
    • Blood
    • Bile
    • Stomach contents
    • Pancreatic contents

    Clinical features

    • Severe and continuous abdominal pain
    • Generalised pain if the whole peritoneum is affected
    • Abdominal swelling or distension
    • Fever
    • Vomiting
    • Tachycardia
    • Hypoxia
    • Hypovolaemic shock
    • Reduced urinary volume
    • Tender, rigid abdomen
    • Rebound tenderness, where pressure on the abdomen followed by sudden release causes sharper pain
    • Absent bowel sounds

    Investigations

    • Abdominal X-ray
    • Abdominal ultrasound
    • Complete blood count
    • Blood culture and sensitivity
    • Renal function tests
    • Electrolytes
    • Liver function tests

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Refer to hospital. H
    Start initial treatment before referral. H
    Monitor temperature. H
    Monitor blood pressure, pulse, SpO₂, and urine output. H
    Put up an IV drip with normal saline, Ringer’s lactate, or any other crystalloid. Give 1 L every 1 to 2 hours until blood pressure is normal, then 1 L every 4 to 6 hours when blood pressure is normal. H
    Keep the patient nil by mouth. H
    Pass a nasogastric tube and start suction. H
    Ask the patient to lie on their side in a comfortable position. H
    Give oxygen if the patient is hypoxic. H
    Give pain control. Avoid NSAIDs. H
    Give pethidine 50 mg IM or IV. H
    Children: give pethidine 0.5 to 2 mg/kg. H
    Alternatively, give morphine 5 to 15 mg IV, IM, or SC. H
    Children: give morphine 2.5 to 5 mg IM, IV, or SC. H
    Refer the patient to hospital for further management, including possible exploratory laparotomy. H

    Management when bacterial infection and fever are suspected

    Give a minimum 7-day antibiotic course.

    Treatment LOC
    Give ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV once daily. H
    Children: ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg per dose. H
    Plus gentamicin 7 mg/kg IV daily in divided doses. H
    Children: gentamicin 2.5 mg/kg every 8 hours. H
    Plus metronidazole 500 mg by IV infusion every 8 hours. Change to metronidazole 400 mg orally every 8 hours when possible. H
    Children: metronidazole 12.5 mg/kg IV per dose. Change to oral route when possible. H
    Identify and control the source of infection. H
    Prevent and control complications through proper nutrition, early ambulation, and rehabilitation. H

    6.1.5 Diarrhoea¶

    ICD-10 CODE: Depending on the cause

    Diarrhoea is defined as three or more loose, watery stools within 24 hours.

    Dysentery is diarrhoea with visible blood and mucus.

    Persistent diarrhoea refers to diarrhoea lasting more than 14 days.

    Causes

    • Viruses, including rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus, measles virus, hepatitis A virus, hepatitis E virus, and Ebola virus
    • Bacteria, including Vibrio cholerae, E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella, and Campylobacter
    • Protozoa, including giardiasis, malaria, and cryptosporidiosis
    • Helminths, including strongyloidiasis and schistosomiasis
    • Infectious diseases, such as measles, malaria, and other fever-causing conditions
    • Malnutrition, such as kwashiorkor
    • Medicines, such as prolonged use of purgatives and broad-spectrum antibiotics
    • Unhygienic feeding methods
    • Malabsorption syndrome
    • Lactose intolerance
    • HIV-associated diarrhoea
    • Irritable bowel syndrome
    • Metabolic conditions, including diabetes and thyroid disease
    • Travellers’ diarrhoea
    • Inflammatory bowel disease, where persistent diarrhoea is usually bloody

    Clinical features

    • Loose watery stools
    • Abdominal cramps
    • Dehydration, presenting with thirst, sunken eyes, loss of skin elasticity, and low urine output
    • Signs of malnutrition if diarrhoea persists for more than 14 days
    • Blood in stool in dysentery

    Investigations

    • Stool microscopy
    • Stool culture and sensitivity
    • Other investigations according to history and physical examination

    Red flags

    • Fever
    • Extremes of age
    • History of travel from a known endemic area
    • Dysentery
    • Shock
    • Failure to feed
    • Mental confusion

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Prevent or correct dehydration with ORS or IV fluids according to treatment Plans A, B, or C. See section 1.1.3. HC2 / HC4
    Find and treat the cause if indicated. HC2 / HC4
    Avoid inappropriate use of antibiotics, such as metronidazole and ciprofloxacin. HC2 / HC4
    Routinely use zinc supplementation in children older than 6 months: 20 mg daily for 10 to 14 days. HC2
    Prevent or treat undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies. HC2 / HC4

    Persistent or chronic diarrhoea

    Treatment LOC
    Adults only: manage as above and add codeine phosphate 30 mg every 8 to 12 hours as required. HC4
    Children: give vitamin A. HC4
    Children 6 to 11 months: vitamin A 100,000 IU. HC4
    Children 1 to 6 years: vitamin A 200,000 IU. HC4

    Prevention

    • Vaccination against measles, rotavirus, polio, and hepatitis A virus
    • Notify suspected epidemics, such as cholera, hepatitis A or E virus infection, and Ebola
    • Encourage handwashing
    • Promote use of clean drinking water
    • Promote proper waste disposal

    6.2 Gastrointestinal Infections¶

    6.2.1 Amoebiasis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: A06

    Amoebiasis is a common parasitic infection of the gastrointestinal system acquired through faeco-oral transmission.

    Cause

    • Entamoeba histolytica

    Clinical features

    Amoebiasis may present as amoebic dysentery or amoebic abscess.

    Amoebic dysentery may present with:

    • Persistent mucoid or bloody diarrhoea
    • Abdominal pain
    • Tenesmus
    • Chronic carrier state, which may be asymptomatic

    Amoebic abscess may occur following spread through the bloodstream and may present as:

    • Liver abscess, with swelling or pain in the right subcostal area, fever, chills, sweating, and weight loss
    • Brain involvement, presenting as a space-occupying lesion
    • Lung involvement, presenting with cough and blood-stained sputum
    • Amoeboma, presenting as a swelling anywhere in the abdomen, especially in the ascending colon
    • Anal ulceration, which may occur by direct extension from intestinal infection

    Differential diagnosis

    • Bacillary dysentery
    • Any other cause of bloody diarrhoea
    • Cancer of the liver
    • Other causes of liver swelling
    • Carcinoma of the colon

    Investigations

    • Stool microscopy for cysts and motile organisms
    • Ultrasound

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Correct any dehydration. See section 1.1.3. HC2
    Give metronidazole 800 mg every 8 hours for 10 days. HC2 / H
    Children: metronidazole 10 mg/kg per dose. HC2 / H
    Alternatively, give tinidazole 2 g daily for 5 days. HC2 / H
    Children: tinidazole 50 mg/kg per dose. HC2 / H

    Caution

    Do not use metronidazole or tinidazole in the first trimester of pregnancy.

    Avoid alcohol during treatment and for 48 hours after completing treatment.

    Metronidazole should be taken after food.

    Prevention

    • Educate the public on personal and food hygiene, including handwashing before eating
    • Promote proper faecal disposal
    • Ensure proper management of carriers and patients
    • Promote use of clean drinking water

    6.2.2 Bacillary Dysentery (Shigellosis)¶

    ICD-10 CODE: A03.9

    Bacillary dysentery is an acute bacterial disease involving the large and small intestine. It is characterised by bloody mucoid diarrhoea.

    Bacillary dysentery is a notifiable disease.

    Cause

    • Shigella dysenteriae
    • Shigella flexneri
    • Shigella sonnei

    These organisms are spread by the faeco-oral route.

    Clinical features

    • Mucoid bloody diarrhoea
    • Fever
    • Nausea
    • Vomiting
    • Abdominal cramps
    • Tenesmus, which is the sensation of desire to defecate without production of significant amounts of faeces
    • Toxaemia in some cases

    S. flexneri infection may be complicated by Reiter’s syndrome, which presents with:

    • Urethritis
    • Conjunctivitis
    • Arthritis

    Differential diagnosis

    • Amoebic dysentery
    • Other causes of bloody diarrhoea

    Investigations

    • Stool culture and sensitivity
    • Stool microscopy

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Correct dehydration using ORS or IV fluids according to the degree of dehydration. See section 1.1.3. HC2 / HC3
    Give appropriate antibiotic treatment according to local sensitivity patterns. HC2 / HC3
    Provide supportive care, including continued feeding and fluid replacement. HC2 / HC3
    Notify and report cases according to national disease surveillance requirements. HC2 / HC3

    Prevention

    Educate the patient and the public on:

    • Rehydration with plenty of fluids
    • Continuing breastfeeding or weaning feeds
    • Personal and food hygiene
    • Washing hands before preparing or eating food
    • Washing hands after using the toilet
    • Using and drinking clean safe water
    • Proper disposal of human faeces
    • Prompt isolation, treatment, and reporting of cases

    6.2.3 Cholera¶

    ICD-10 CODE: A00

    Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal disease characterised by profuse watery diarrhoea and rapid dehydration. It is a notifiable disease.

    Cause

    • Vibrio cholerae, spread mainly through contaminated water and food

    Clinical features

    • Profuse watery diarrhoea, often described as rice-water stool
    • Vomiting
    • Rapid dehydration
    • Thirst
    • Sunken eyes
    • Reduced skin turgor
    • Weak pulse
    • Hypotension
    • Shock in severe cases
    • Muscle cramps may occur

    Differential diagnosis

    • Other causes of acute watery diarrhoea
    • Food poisoning
    • Viral gastroenteritis

    Investigations

    • Stool culture and sensitivity
    • Stool microscopy
    • Identification of mobile vibrios under microscope where available

    Management

    Up to 90% of patients with cholera only require prompt oral rehydration. Only severely dehydrated patients need IV fluids and antimicrobials.

    Treatment LOC
    Start rehydration with ORS at HC1 or HC2 and refer for isolation. HC2
    Give oral fluids using ORS, or IV fluids using Ringer’s lactate, according to the degree of dehydration. See section 1.1.3. HC2 / HC3
    Give IV glucose for hypoglycaemia. HC3
    Give maintenance fluids of at least 4 to 5 litres per day in adults, adjusted according to clinical condition. HC3
    Give doxycycline 300 mg as a single dose. HC3
    Children: doxycycline 4 mg/kg as a single dose. HC3
    Alternatively, give erythromycin 25 to 50 mg/kg every 6 hours for 3 days in children below 12 years. HC3
    Alternatively, give ciprofloxacin 1 g as a single dose. HC3
    Children: ciprofloxacin 20 mg/kg every 12 hours for 3 days. HC3

    Caution

    Ciprofloxacin and doxycycline are usually contraindicated in pregnancy and in children below 8 years.

    However, a single dose in cholera should not provoke adverse effects.

    Alternative treatment: erythromycin 500 mg every 6 hours for 5 days.

    Prevention

    Educate the patient and the public on:

    • Rehydration with plenty of fluids
    • Continuing breastfeeding or weaning feeds
    • Personal and food hygiene
    • Washing hands before preparing or eating food
    • Washing hands after using the toilet
    • Using and drinking clean safe water
    • Proper disposal of human faeces
    • Prompt isolation, treatment, and reporting of cases

    6.2.4 Giardiasis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: A07.1

    Giardiasis is a protozoan infection of the upper small intestine transmitted by the faeco-oral route.

    Cause

    • Giardia lamblia, a flagellated protozoan

    Clinical features

    • Often asymptomatic
    • Prolonged diarrhoea
    • Steatorrhoea
    • Abdominal cramps
    • Bloating
    • Fatigue
    • Weight loss
    • Malabsorption of fats and fat-soluble vitamins

    Severe giardiasis may cause:

    • Reactive arthritis
    • Damage to duodenal and jejunal mucosa

    Differential diagnosis

    • Other causes of prolonged diarrhoea
    • Other causes of malabsorption

    Investigations

    • Stool microscopy for cysts and trophozoites

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Give metronidazole 2 g after food once daily for 3 days. HC2 / H
    Children: metronidazole 30 mg/kg per dose, maximum 1.2 g. HC2 / H
    Alternatively, give tinidazole 2 g as a single dose. HC2 / H
    Children: tinidazole 50 mg/kg as a single dose. HC2 / H

    Caution

    Avoid metronidazole and tinidazole in the first trimester of pregnancy.

    Avoid alcohol during treatment and for 48 hours after treatment.

    Metronidazole should be taken after food.

    Prevention

    Provide health education on:

    • Personal and food hygiene
    • Washing hands before handling or eating food
    • Washing hands after using toilets
    • Proper disposal of human faeces
    • Use of safe clean drinking water

    6.3 Gastrointestinal Disorders¶

    6.3.1 Dysphagia¶

    ICD-10 CODE: R13.1

    Dysphagia is difficulty in swallowing. It may be oropharyngeal dysphagia or oesophageal dysphagia.

    Causes

    Oropharyngeal dysphagia may be caused by:

    • Neurological conditions, including stroke, Parkinson’s disease, dementia, multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia, cerebral palsy, tardive dyskinesia, brain tumours, and trauma
    • Myopathy, including connective tissue diseases, sarcoidosis, and dermatomyositis
    • Structural causes, including Zenker’s diverticulum, webs, oropharyngeal tumours, and osteophytes
    • Infections, including syphilis, botulism, rabies, and mucositis
    • Metabolic conditions, including Cushing’s syndrome, thyrotoxicosis, and Wilson’s disease
    • Iatrogenic causes, including chemotherapy, neuroleptics, post-surgery complications, and post-radiation complications

    Oesophageal dysphagia may be caused by:

    • Tumours, including cancer of the oesophagus
    • Oesophagitis, including gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, candidiasis, pill oesophagitis such as from doxycycline, and caustic soda injury
    • Extrinsic compression from tumours or lymph nodes
    • Motility disorders, including achalasia, scleroderma, and oesophageal spasms

    Clinical presentation

    • Difficulty initiating a swallow
    • Repetitive swallowing
    • Nasal regurgitation
    • Coughing
    • Nasal speech
    • Drooling
    • Diminished cough reflex
    • Choking
    • Aspiration, which may occur without concurrent choking or coughing
    • Dysarthria and diplopia, which may accompany neurological conditions causing oropharyngeal dysphagia
    • Halitosis in patients with a large residue-containing Zenker’s diverticulum, advanced achalasia, or long-term obstruction with luminal accumulation of decomposing residue
    • Recurrent pneumonia
    • Other features depending on the underlying cause

    Investigations

    • Medical history and physical examination
    • Timed water swallow test, complemented by a food test
    • Endoscopy, which is mandatory
    • HIV serology
    • Random blood sugar
    • Electrolytes

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Ensure rehydration with IV fluids. HC3
    Prevent malnutrition through appropriate energy replacement. HC3
    Treat the cause where possible, for example fluconazole trial in suspected oral candidiasis among HIV patients. HC3
    Consult and/or refer the patient. HC3

    6.3.2 Dyspepsia¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K30

    Dyspepsia is upper abdominal discomfort arising from the upper gastrointestinal tract, usually lasting more than 2 to 4 weeks.

    Causes

    • Peptic ulcer disease
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD)
    • Functional dyspepsia
    • Gastric or oesophageal cancer
    • Oesophagitis, including drug-induced oesophagitis, candida oesophagitis, and other causes
    • Gastroparesis or gastric outlet obstruction
    • Other motility disorders

    Clinical features

    • Epigastric pain or discomfort
    • Heartburn
    • Bloating
    • Early satiety
    • Fullness after meals
    • Repeated belching or regurgitation, often rumination
    • Nausea

    Dyspepsia alarm features requiring endoscopy and referral

    Refer for endoscopy if any of the following are present:

    • Dysphagia
    • Odynophagia among patients who are HIV negative
    • Weight loss
    • Abdominal mass
    • Cervical lymphadenopathy
    • Evidence of upper gastrointestinal bleeding
    • Iron deficiency anaemia
    • Recurrent vomiting
    • Recent dyspeptic symptoms or new dyspepsia in individuals over 40 years of age

    Other indications for endoscopy

    • History of long-term smoking and alcohol misuse
    • Persistent dyspepsia despite appropriate treatment, such as proton-pump inhibitors in GERD
    • Hepatobiliary disease

    6.3.4 Gastritis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K27

    Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining. It may be acute or chronic.

    Causes

    Acute gastritis may be caused by:

    • Medicines such as acetylsalicylic acid, diclofenac, and ibuprofen
    • Alcohol
    • Regurgitation of bile into the stomach

    Chronic gastritis may be caused by:

    • Autoimmune gastric ulceration
    • Bacterial infection, especially Helicobacter pylori

    Clinical features

    Gastritis may be asymptomatic or may present with:

    • Anorexia
    • Nausea
    • Epigastric pain
    • Heartburn

    Differential diagnosis

    • Pancreatitis
    • Cholecystitis
    • Peptic ulcer disease
    • Duodenal ulcers
    • Cancer of the stomach
    • Epigastric hernia

    Investigations

    • Gastroscopy
    • Stool test for occult blood
    • Barium meal for chronic gastritis

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Modify diet by avoiding precipitating causes and increasing milk intake. HC2
    Give an antacid, such as magnesium trisilicate compound 2 tablets every 8 hours as required. HC3
    If there is no response, give omeprazole 20 mg in the evening for 4 weeks. HC4
    If vomiting occurs, give metoclopramide 10 mg IM, repeated when necessary up to 3 times daily. HC4

    • Avoid tobacco
    • Avoid alcohol
    • Avoid carbonated drinks

    6.3.5 Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)¶

    Peptic ulcer disease refers to ulceration in the stomach or duodenum.

    Causes

    • Helicobacter pylori infection
    • Hyperacidity due to medicines such as NSAIDs, including acetylsalicylic acid, diclofenac, ibuprofen, and corticosteroids
    • Irregular meals
    • Stress
    • Alcohol
    • Smoking
    • Caffeine-containing beverages

    Clinical features

    General features include:

    • Epigastric pain, typically worse at night and when hungry in duodenal ulcer
    • Pain relieved by food, milk, or antacid medicine in duodenal ulcer
    • Epigastric pain that is worse with food in gastric ulcer
    • Vomiting
    • Nausea
    • Regurgitation
    • Discomfort on palpation of the upper abdomen

    Bleeding ulcer may present with:

    • Haematemesis, which may be coffee-brown or red vomitus
    • Black stools, also called melaena
    • Sudden weakness and dizziness
    • Cold, clammy skin when the patient has lost a lot of blood

    Perforated ulcer may present with:

    • Acute abdominal pain
    • Signs of peritonitis, such as a rigid abdomen
    • Ground coffee-brown vomitus due to blood
    • Fever
    • Shock, with weak pulse, clammy skin, and low blood pressure

    Differential diagnosis

    • Pancreatitis
    • Hepatitis
    • Disease of the aorta
    • Myocardial infarction
    • Lung disease, especially haemoptysis

    Investigations

    • Stool antigen test for H. pylori. This is used for diagnosis and to confirm eradication.
    • Gastroscopy
    • Biopsy of the stomach wall
    • Barium meal

    Warning

    The H. pylori stool antigen test may give a false negative result if the patient has taken antibiotics or omeprazole in the previous 2 weeks.

    Serum antibody testing is not useful for diagnosis or follow-up.

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Modify diet by avoiding precipitating causes and increasing milk intake. HC3
    Give an antacid, such as magnesium trisilicate compound 2 tablets every 8 hours as required. HC4

    Treatment for eradication of H. pylori

    Treatment LOC
    First-line triple therapy: give amoxicillin 1 g every 12 hours plus metronidazole 400 mg every 12 hours plus omeprazole 20 mg every 12 hours for 2 weeks. HC3
    Check eradication with a stool antigen test after 4 weeks. HC3

    Management of bleeding or perforated ulcer

    Treatment LOC
    Refer the patient to hospital immediately. H
    Give IV fluids and blood if necessary. H
    Give IV ranitidine 50 mg in 20 mL slowly every 8 hours. H

    Note

    Tinidazole 500 mg every 12 hours can be used instead of metronidazole.

    Confirm eradication with a stool antigen test 1 month after completion of treatment. The test should be negative.

    6.3.6 Chronic Pancreatitis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K86.0–K86.1

    Chronic pancreatitis is a disease of the pancreas in which recurrent episodes of inflammation lead to replacement of the pancreatic parenchyma with fibrotic connective tissue, formation of calculi, and loss of duct architecture. This leads to progressive loss of pancreatic function.

    Causes

    • Toxic or metabolic causes, including alcohol, tobacco, hypercalcaemia, hyperlipidaemia, and chronic renal failure
    • Idiopathic causes, including tropical pancreatitis
    • Genetic or autoimmune causes
    • Recurrent and severe acute pancreatitis
    • Obstructive cancer or anatomical abnormalities

    Clinical features

    • Chronic pain, which is the main symptom
    • Diarrhoea
    • Loss of weight
    • Diabetes mellitus

    Complications

    • Pseudocysts
    • Stenosis of the pancreatic duct
    • Duodenal stenosis
    • Vascular complications
    • Compression of the bile ducts
    • Malnutrition
    • Increased risk of cancer of the pancreas

    Investigations

    • Blood tests, including serum analysis, complete blood count, and random blood sugar
    • Raised pancreatic amylase or lipase above 3 times normal
    • Ultrasound, looking for gallstones, pancreatic oedema, and abdominal fluid
    • Liver function tests, which may show raised liver enzymes

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Refer for specialist management. RR
    Use the WHO pain analgesic ladder. RR
    Give pethidine 50–100 mg IM as required. RR
    Alternatively, give tramadol 50–100 mg orally or IM as required. RR
    Advise the patient to avoid alcohol and fatty foods. RR

    6.4 Anorectal Disorders¶

    6.4.1 Constipation¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K59.0

    Constipation is a condition characterised by hardened faeces and difficulty emptying the bowels.

    Causes

    • Dietary causes, including lack of roughage and inadequate fluid intake
    • Concentrated feeds in infants
    • Lack of exercise
    • Being bedridden, especially in elderly patients
    • Pregnancy
    • Certain medicines, including narcotic analgesics, antidepressants, diuretics, antipsychotics, and iron
    • Colon or anorectal disorders, including stricture, cancer, fissure, proctitis, congenital bowel abnormalities, irritable bowel syndrome, volvulus, and intussusception
    • Metabolic causes, including hypercalcaemia, diabetes, and hypothyroidism
    • Neurological disorders, including spinal cord lesions, stroke, and Parkinsonism

    Clinical features

    • Abdominal discomfort
    • Small hard stools passed irregularly under strain
    • Haemorrhoids
    • Anal fissure

    Alarm features

    • Symptoms and signs of intestinal obstruction or acute abdomen
    • Confusion or disorientation
    • Abnormal vital signs
    • Iron deficiency
    • Rectal bleeding, haematochezia, or rectal mass
    • Haem-positive stool
    • Age above 45 years with no previous history of colon cancer screening
    • History of colon cancer in immediate family relatives
    • Weight loss

    Investigations

    Physical examination may include:

    • Assessment for abdominal mass and tenderness
    • Anorectal examination for faecal impaction, stricture, rectal prolapse, or rectal mass

    Other investigations may include:

    • Stool examination

    For patients with alarm features, consider:

    • Abdominal series, including supine, upright, and left lateral decubitus views
    • Transabdominal ultrasound
    • Endoscopy
    • Complete blood count
    • Renal function tests
    • Serum calcium
    • Thyroid function tests
    • Blood sugar
    • Barium enema, with or without CT scan or X-ray

    Management

    Clinical situation Treatment LOC
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Advise high dietary fibre intake. HC2
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Advise adequate fluid intake. HC2
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Give bisacodyl 10 mg at night in adults. Continue until stool is passed. HC3
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Children 5 to 12 years: bisacodyl 5 mg, suppository only. HC3
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Bisacodyl is contraindicated in acute abdomen because it aggravates the condition. HC3
    No alarm features or chronic constipation Give oral or rectal lactulose as an osmotic agent. It provides faster relief than bisacodyl. HC4
    Alarm features or severe chronic constipation Refer to hospital for specialist management. HC2 / HC3

    Prevention

    • Eat a diet rich in roughage, including plenty of vegetables and fruit.
    • Take plenty of oral fluids with meals.
    • Increase exercise.

    6.4.2 Haemorrhoids (Piles) and Anal Fissures¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K64 / K60.0–K60.2

    Haemorrhoids are swellings in the upper anal canal and lower rectum due to engorgement of veins. They may be internal or external.

    An anal fissure is a tear in the lining of the lower rectum.

    Causes

    • Constipation and straining during defecation
    • Portal hypertension from any cause
    • Compression of pelvic veins, such as from abdominal tumours or pregnancy
    • Sedentary lifestyle

    Clinical features

    Haemorrhoids may present with:

    • Painless rectal bleeding
    • Visible swelling at the anus or prolapse of the swelling, especially at defecation
    • Blood that is usually not mixed with stool but instead coats the surface of the stool or toilet paper
    • Mucous discharge and irritation at the anus

    Anal fissure may present with:

    • Pain during passing stool
    • Bleeding during passing stool

    Differential diagnosis

    • Schistosomiasis
    • Amoeboma
    • Rectal polyps
    • Prolapsed rectum
    • Anal tags
    • Harmless growths that hang off the skin around the outside of the anus
    • Tumour of the rectum
    • Anal warts

    Investigations

    • Visual inspection and digital rectal examination
    • Proctoscopy
    • Sigmoidoscopy
    • Colonoscopy

    Management

    Clinical situation Treatment LOC
    General management Establish the cause. HC2
    General management Increase fibre and fluid intake. HC2
    General management Correct any constipation. HC2
    General management Give a sitz bath, sitting for 10 to 15 minutes in lukewarm water with a spoon of salt, 2 or 3 times daily. HC4
    General management Insert a bismuth subgallate compound rectally every 12 hours for 5 days, such as Anusol or Sediproct cream or suppositories. HC4
    If signs of infection are present Give metronidazole 400 mg every 8 hours for 5 days. HC2
    If signs of infection are present Give analgesics as required for pain. HC2
    If there is no response Refer to hospital for surgery. HC2

    Prevention

    • Maintain a high-residue, fibre-rich diet.
    • Ensure adequate fluid intake.
    • Exercise regularly.
    • Refrain from straining and reading in the toilet.

    6.5 Hepatic and Biliary Diseases¶

    6.5.1 Viral Hepatitis¶

    Viral hepatitis is inflammation of the liver caused by hepatitis viruses. It may present as acute hepatitis, with or without symptoms. Hepatitis B, C, and D viruses can cause chronic hepatitis. Hepatitis B virus can also result in chronic carrier status.

    Causes

    • Hepatitis A and E: oro-faecal transmission
    • Hepatitis B: sexual transmission, mother-to-child transmission, or transmission through infected body fluids or blood
    • Hepatitis C: contact with infected blood, with possible sexual and vertical transmission
    • Hepatitis D: contact with infected blood, sexual transmission, and possible vertical transmission

    6.5.1.1 Acute Hepatitis¶

    ICD-10 CODES: B15, B16, B17, B19

    Clinical features

    Classic acute hepatitis may present with:

    • Fever
    • Fatigue
    • Malaise
    • Right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort
    • Nausea
    • Diarrhoea
    • Anorexia
    • Jaundice
    • Dark urine
    • Pale or clay-coloured stool

    Fulminant hepatitis may present as acute liver failure due to massive liver necrosis. It is often fatal. It is more common in patients with hepatitis B who have secondary infection with hepatitis D virus, and in pregnant women who get hepatitis E in the third trimester.

    Differential diagnosis

    • Other causes of hepatitis, including drugs, herbs, tumours, and autoimmune diseases
    • Gastroenteritis
    • Relapsing fever
    • Pancreatitis
    • Malaria
    • Leptospirosis
    • Yellow fever
    • Haemorrhagic fevers, such as Marburg and Ebola

    Investigations

    • Complete blood count
    • Malaria slide or rapid diagnostic test
    • Liver function tests
    • Viral antigens and antibodies, including hepatitis B and hepatitis C testing
    • HIV serology

    Management

    Clinical form Treatment LOC
    Classic form Provide supportive management. HC4
    Classic form Advise rest and hydration. HC4
    Classic form Give a diet high in carbohydrates, vitamins, and vegetable proteins. Avoid animal proteins. HC4
    Classic form Avoid unnecessary medicines because they may aggravate symptoms. HC4
    Liver failure or decompensated liver disease Refer for further management. HC4

    Caution

    Avoid medicines generally, especially sedatives and hepatotoxic medicines.

    Ensure effective infection control measures, including barrier nursing and personal hygiene.

    Patient isolation is not necessary unless there is high suspicion of viral haemorrhagic fever.

    Prevention

    • Promote hygiene and sanitation.
    • Provide immunisation against hepatitis B for all children, health workers, household contacts of people with chronic hepatitis B, sex workers, and other populations at risk.
    • Promote safe transfusion practices.
    • Strengthen infection control in health facilities.
    • Screen pregnant women.
    • Promote safer sexual practices, including condom use.

    6.5.1.2 Chronic Hepatitis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: B18

    Hepatitis B, C, and D viruses can cause chronic infection with low-level inflammation of the liver. This may lead to progressive liver damage and may progress to liver cirrhosis.

    6.5.2 Hepatitis B Special Situations¶

    6.5.2.1 Inactive Hepatitis B Carriers¶

    ICD-10 CODE: B18.1

    A patient may be classified as an inactive hepatitis B carrier if the following are present:

    • HBsAg positive for more than 6 months
    • Persistently normal liver function, at least 3 times in 12 months
    • No evidence of viral replication, shown by negative HBeAg and/or HBV DNA below 2,000 IU/mL

    Patients classified as inactive carriers should be monitored once a year with:

    • Complete blood count
    • Renal function tests
    • Liver function tests
    • HBsAg
    • Abdominal ultrasound

    If possible, HBV DNA should be done every 3 years.

    Inactive carriers are not highly infectious, but close contacts should be immunised and appropriate precautions should be followed.

    6.5.2.2 Pregnant Mother HBsAg Positive¶

    ICD-10 CODE: B18.1

    If a pregnant mother is found to be HBsAg positive, assess HIV status.

    If she is also HIV positive:

    • Start antiretroviral therapy.
    • The child should receive hepatitis B vaccine at birth.

    If she is HIV negative:

    • Refer for further testing, including HBeAg and HBV DNA, to assess the risk of transmission to the baby and the possible need for antivirals.
    • The child should be immunised at birth.

    Breastfeeding is safe.

    6.5.4 Liver Cirrhosis¶

    ICD-10 CODES: K74, K70.3

    Cirrhosis is a chronic disease with necrosis of liver cells followed by fibrosis and nodule formation. Decompensated cirrhosis is defined by the presence of complications such as ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy, or jaundice which result from portal hypertension and liver insufficiency caused by cirrhosis.

    Causes

    • Infections, such as viral hepatitis B and D, and hepatitis C
    • Intoxication with alcohol, medicines, or toxins, such as methotrexate, isoniazid, and methyldopa
    • Infiltrative disorders, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Wilson’s disease, and haemochromatosis
    • Iron overload, such as in over-transfused sickle cell disease patients
    • Immunological causes, including chronic autoimmune hepatitis
    • Congestion with bile, such as primary biliary cirrhosis
    • Congestion with blood, such as chronic cardiac failure and Budd-Chiari syndrome
    • Idiopathic causes

    Clinical features

    General symptoms may include:

    • Fatigue
    • Weight loss
    • Features of malnutrition
    • Nausea
    • Vomiting
    • Loss of appetite

    Other clinical features may include:

    • Initially enlarged liver, which later decreases in size
    • Distended blood vessels on the abdomen
    • Enlarged spleen
    • Loss of libido

    Cirrhosis is decompensated when the following are present:

    • Jaundice
    • Encephalopathy
    • Ascites, with or without leg oedema
    • Vomiting of blood from ruptured blood vessels in the oesophagus, or varices

    Stages of cirrhosis

    Stage Clinical status Death at 1 year
    0 Fibrosis 1%
    1 Compensated cirrhosis, with no varices and no ascites 1%
    2 Varices present, with no ascites 3%
    3 Decompensated cirrhosis with ascites, with or without varices 20%
    4 Decompensated cirrhosis with bleeding, with or without ascites 57%

    Additional complications may include:

    • Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis with sepsis
    • Renal failure
    • Hepatocellular carcinoma
    • Jaundice
    • Hepatic encephalopathy

    Differential diagnosis

    • Diffuse hepatic parenchymal disease
    • Metastatic or multifocal cancer in the liver
    • Hepatic vein obstruction
    • Any cause of enlarged spleen
    • Heart failure
    • Renal disease

    Investigations

    • Blood haemoglobin
    • Blood film
    • White blood cell count
    • Platelet count
    • Prothrombin time or INR
    • Serology for hepatitis B, C, and D
    • HIV serology
    • Stool and urine examination
    • Abdominal ultrasound
    • Liver function tests
    • Alpha-fetoprotein
    • Liver biopsy
    • APRI score greater than 2 is diagnostic
    • Endoscopy to assess for varices

    Management

    Refer to a regional hospital or higher for specialist attention.

    Management area Treatment LOC
    General principles Treat the cause and prevent progression. RR
    General principles Stop alcohol. RR
    General principles Ensure appropriate nutrition. RR
    General principles If chronic hepatitis B is present, start antiviral treatment. RR
    General principles Provide specific treatment according to the cause. RR
    General principles Avoid herbs and self-medication. RR
    General principles Use medicines only after prescription from a health worker. RR
    General principles Manage and prevent complications. RR
    Complications Manage ascites. RR
    Complications Manage encephalopathy. RR
    Complications Manage bleeding varices. RR

    6.5.4.1 Ascites¶

    ICD-10 CODES: R18 / K70.31 / K70.11 / K71.51

    Ascites is the pathological accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity.

    Clinical features

    • Ascites not infected and not associated with hepatorenal syndrome

    Classification

    Classification Features
    Grade 1 ascites (mild) Only detectable by ultrasound examination.
    Grade 2 ascites (moderate) Ascites causing moderate symmetrical distension of the abdomen.
    Grade 3 ascites (severe) Ascites causing marked abdominal distension.

    Clinical diagnosis

    • Fluid thrill or fluid wave
    • Shifting dullness

    Investigations

    • Abdominal ultrasound scan
    • Peritoneal tap or paracentesis
    • Analysis of ascitic fluid

    Management

    The main principles of management are:

    • Diet modification
    • Daily monitoring
    • Diuretics
    • Drainage
    Management area Treatment LOC
    Diet Restrict dietary salt to a no-added-salt or low-salt diet. H
    Diet Avoid protein malnutrition because it is associated with higher mortality. Consume plant proteins liberally and animal proteins occasionally, titrated to symptoms and signs of hepatic encephalopathy. H
    Diet Restrict water if oedema and hyponatraemia are present. H
    Diet Abstain from alcohol, NSAIDs, and herbs. H
    Daily monitoring Monitor daily weight, blood pressure, pulse, stool for melaena, and encephalopathy. H
    Diuretics Use spironolactone 50 to 100 mg/day in the morning, aiming for weight loss of 300 to 500 g/day. If needed, increase the dose every 7 days up to a maximum of 400 mg/day. H
    Diuretics Furosemide may be added at a starting dose of 20 to 40 mg/day and subsequently increased to 160 mg/day if needed. It is best used if pedal oedema is present. Monitor for hypotension. H
    Diuretics For maintenance, titrate to the lowest effective diuretic dose. Most patients do well with spironolactone 50 mg/day if they have no ascites. H
    Drainage Drainage is indicated for severe ascites, Grade 3. Paracentesis is always followed by spironolactone. H
    Drainage Small-volume paracentesis is less than 5 L over 3 to 4 hours. Large-volume paracentesis is 5 to 10 L with infusion of a plasma expander, such as 8 g albumin per litre of ascites removed. H
    Drainage Monitor for hypotension or reduced urine output. H
    Complicated ascites Refer if the patient has or develops complicated ascites. H

    6.5.4.2 Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K65.2

    Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis is an acute bacterial infection of ascitic fluid. It is a common and severe complication of advanced liver cirrhosis and is associated with poor prognosis.

    Clinical features

    Patients must be admitted to hospital. Suspect spontaneous bacterial peritonitis when there is:

    • Increasing severity of ascites
    • Fever
    • Abdominal pain or abdominal tenderness
    • Worsening encephalopathy
    • Renal failure
    • Bleeding varices

    Investigations

    • Ascitic tap
    • Ascitic fluid cell count

    A neutrophil count above 250/mm³ in ascitic fluid confirms the diagnosis.

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Treat with IV antibiotics for 5 to 10 days. H
    Give ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g IV daily. H
    If needed, add metronidazole 500 mg IV every 8 hours. H
    Give albumin infusion 1 g/kg to prevent hepatorenal syndrome. RR
    Consult or refer for specialist care as soon as possible. RR

    Caution

    Avoid gentamicin and NSAIDs.

    6.5.4.3 Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)¶

    ICD-10 CODES: K70.41 / K71.11 / K72.11 / K72.91

    Hepatic encephalopathy is a syndrome of neuropsychiatric symptoms and signs, including coma, observed in patients with cirrhosis. It is probably due to accumulation of toxins in the blood.

    Clinical features

    Grade Clinical features
    Grade 0 Subclinical personality changes and construction apraxia, meaning inability or difficulty to build, assemble, or draw objects.
    Grade I Confusion and flap tremor.
    Grade II Drowsiness.
    Grade III Stupor.
    Grade IV Coma.

    Hepatic encephalopathy may be aggravated by:

    • Surgery
    • Paracentesis
    • Excessive diuretics
    • Sedatives
    • Opioid analgesics

    Intracranial hypertension and sepsis are the main causes of death.

    Management

    Management involves addressing pathophysiological mechanisms related to the brain, gut, and liver.

    Treatment LOC
    Identify and correct precipitating factors, including renal impairment, gastrointestinal bleeding, infections, and electrolyte disturbances. H
    Empty the gut. H
    Give oral lactulose 15 to 30 mL every 8 hours until the condition resolves. Aim for 2 to 3 soft stools per day. H
    Lactulose may be administered through a nasogastric tube in Grade I and Grade II hepatic encephalopathy. H
    Lactulose may be given as an enema in acute hepatic encephalopathy Grade III and Grade IV. H
    Refer to a specialist. RR
    If referral delays, give an antibiotic with local action on the gut, such as oral metronidazole 400 to 800 mg every 8 hours for 5 days. RR
    Alternatively, give oral paromomycin 1000 mg every 6 hours for 5 days. RR

    6.5.6 Drug-Induced Liver Injury¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K71

    Drugs are an important and common cause of liver injury. Many medicines and herbs are known to cause liver damage. Drug-induced liver injury can range from asymptomatic elevation of liver enzymes to severe hepatic failure.

    Health workers must be vigilant in identifying drug-related liver injury because early detection can decrease the severity of hepatotoxicity if the drug is discontinued. Knowledge of the commonly implicated agents is essential in diagnosis.

    Common causes

    • Phenytoin
    • Carbamazepine
    • Anti-tuberculosis drugs
    • Co-trimoxazole
    • Diclofenac
    • Paracetamol
    • Antiretroviral drugs
    • Ketoconazole

    Clinical features

    Drug-induced liver injury is a diagnosis of exclusion. Consider it in:

    • Any patient with liver enzyme elevation that cannot be attributed to infections, autoimmune disease, or malignancy
    • A patient exposed to a drug or herbal medication known to cause liver cell injury
    • A patient who presents with skin or mucosal drug reactions, such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Stop all drugs or herbs. H
    Give supportive care, including rehydration. H
    See section 1.3.5 for paracetamol poisoning. H
    Do not give the drug again. Do not rechallenge. H
    Refer to a regional hospital or higher level of care for specialist attention. H

    6.5.7 Jaundice (Hyperbilirubinaemia)¶

    ICD-10 CODE: R17

    Jaundice is yellowish discolouration of the sclera and skin due to raised levels of bilirubin in the body. Bilirubin is a by-product of red cell breakdown, processed in the liver and excreted mainly in bile. Jaundice may be benign or life-threatening.

    Causes

    • Pre-hepatic causes, including haemolysis, sepsis, sickle cell disease, pregnancy-related HELLP syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and vascular causes
    • Hepatic causes, including hepatitis, drugs, tumours, alcohol, toxins, herbs, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, and cholangitis
    • Post-hepatic causes, including gall stones, strictures, tumours, surgery, pancreatitis, and biliary disease

    Complications

    • Renal failure
    • Coagulopathy
    • Sepsis

    Investigations

    • Liver function tests, including AST, ALT, and bilirubin
    • Coombs’ test
    • Low haptoglobin
    • LDH
    • Hepatitis A, B, and C testing
    • Malaria and sickle cell screening
    • Ultrasound to assess for dilated bile ducts and gall bladder
    • CBC
    • INR
    • RFTs
    • LDH
    • Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)
    • Liver biopsy

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Refer and/or consult as appropriate. H
    Treat the underlying cause. H
    Discontinue offending factors. H
    Use phototherapy with UV light for newborn babies. H
    Expose newborn babies to natural sun where appropriate. H

    6.5.9 Acute Cholecystitis / Cholangitis¶

    ICD-10 CODE: K81

    Acute cholecystitis or cholangitis is inflammation of the gall bladder and/or biliary tract. It often requires surgical management.

    Causes

    • Obstruction of the gall bladder duct by gall stones or calculi
    • May occur after major trauma, burns, or surgery
    • May occur in HIV-infected persons as acalculous cholecystitis

    Clinical features

    • Sudden onset of pain and tenderness in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen
    • Pain worsens on deep breathing
    • Nausea and vomiting
    • Jaundice in cholangitis
    • Fever, 38–39 °C, with chills

    Severity of acute cholecystitis

    Grade Description
    Grade I, mild acute cholecystitis Associated with no organ dysfunction and limited disease in the gallbladder, making cholecystectomy a low-risk procedure.
    Grade II, moderate acute cholecystitis Associated with no organ dysfunction but with extensive disease in the gallbladder, resulting in difficulty safely performing cholecystectomy.
    Grade II features Usually characterised by elevated white blood cell count, palpable tender mass in the right upper abdominal quadrant, disease duration of more than 72 hours, and imaging studies indicating significant inflammatory changes in the gallbladder.
    Severe acute cholecystitis Acute cholecystitis with organ dysfunction or shock.

    Differential diagnosis

    • Acute alcoholic hepatitis
    • Intestinal obstruction

    Investigations

    • X-ray
    • Abdominal ultrasound, with findings such as wall thickening, stone, and pericholecystic fluid
    • Blood haemogram
    • Liver function tests
    • Pancreatitis assessment
    • Enzymes and renal function tests

    Findings may include fever and elevated white blood cells.

    Management

    Treatment LOC
    Keep nil by mouth. HC4
    Relieve pain with pethidine 50 to 100 mg IM every 6 hours. HC4
    Rehydrate with IV fluids and electrolytes, such as Ringer’s lactate. RR
    Give ceftriaxone 1 to 2 g daily. RR
    In cholecystitis, refer to hospital within 2 to 3 days for surgery, including cholecystectomy. RR
    In cholangitis, if not better, refer for urgent surgical management. RR
